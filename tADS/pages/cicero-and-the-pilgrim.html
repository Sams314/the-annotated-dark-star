Cicero the Silent and the Pilgrim

(Back to home page.)

A Dungeons & Dragons character sketch, ca. 2003.

Now it came to pass, as he went forth from Nargothrond, that Cicero the Silent met an old man on the road.

“Greetings, my lord. Who art thou?” The old man said, as he approached. He held in his left hand a wooden staff, and in his right a brass lantern. He wore a long brown cloak. The knight did not speak, but reached into his coat pocket, and gave the old man a neatly folded scrap of parchment.

Well met. My name is Cicero the Silent. I seek to do good and serve.

The old man looked down at the paper, then up at Cicero again. He nodded in approval.

“‘Tis fine hand-writing, my lord,” was the first thing he said.

Cicero’s face did not move.

“Well, I am oft called the Pilgrim. My quest is this: I seek the Honest Man. Wouldst thou sit with me in the shade of that tree yonder?”

Cicero nodded, and descended from his saddle. The two men left the country road, and trudging through the tall grass they came to a pine tree at the bank of a stream, and they rested among the sedges. Cicero took from his bag a meal of salted tack and rabbit. He gave some to the Pilgrim. They sat awhile in silence. Eventually the Pilgrim spoke.

"Where is thy tongue, my lord?

Cicero did not answer.

"I would fain hear thy tale," the Pilgrim insisted.

Then the knight produced once more his parchment, and wrote for the Pilgrim a short message.

I am a man of Tol, of noble birth. Great is the love of decorated speech among the multitudes of that city, and I spoke well, and many wicked words fell from my mouth ere I saddled my horse and forsook them. I hope thou canst forgive my silence.

The Pilgrim took the parchment, and read it. His face revealed little to Cicero, who watched him, for the old man was strong of mind and quiet of heart. Eventually he nodded and smiled to himself, and gave the note back to the knight, who put it away.

“It is the Honest Man I seek, Silent One. No falseness shall touch his lips, but only a pure and gentle heart he shall bear.”

He took from his bag a worn cloth, and began to polish his lantern. Cicero offered the Pilgrim another bite of rabbit. The old man took it gratefully.

"Thou art a fine fellow," he said between bites.

Then they spent another hour there underneath the tree, sitting once again in silence. It was late afternoon when the two travellers parted, and the rose summer sun lit Cicero from behind and above as he kissed the Pilgrim’s hand which was sweaty and broken and smelled like ash, and as the Pilgrim bowed in farewell.
