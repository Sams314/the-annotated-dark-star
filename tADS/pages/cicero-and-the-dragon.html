Cicero the Silent and the Dragon of Saxony

(Back to home page.)

In the spring of his fourth year of wandering, Cicero the Silent went east, and came to the hill-country of Saxony. Now Saxony was in those days a rough and fair kingdom, and within her borders were many wide forests and meadows. He wandered for some time through the wild country. He saw but few cottages, and those quiet. No smoke rose from their chimneys. His nights he spent in solitude, and though he knew not wherefore, yet he thought he felt fear in the land.

Cicero had been travelling through Saxony for a fortnight (for when he entered the kingdom the moon was slender, but now it was fat and round,) when one evening upon a hill he espied a great and mighty tower above the trees, and a wooden palisade did encircle it. With haste he went forth, and came anon to its gates. Two guardsmen were standing at the door, and one called out to him as he approached.

“Aye, traveller! What business hast thou with the King?”

Cicero reached into his coat pocket and gave him a folded square scrap of parchment. The guardsman took it from him.

Well met. My name is Cicero the Silent. I seek to do good and serve.

The guardsman bowed. “Forgive my crude speech, my lord. I did not see you clearly. Come with me, if it pleases you.”

He opened the gates and led Cicero into the King’s Village, a healthy place in those days, though not as large as it became in latter times. Children dashed across the long street, and no few curious eyes did follow the guardsman leading that brave knight up the hill over the wooden bridge and into the tower.

“You may sit here, my lord. I will tell the steward of your arrival.”

In a few minutes, the guardsman returned and hastily led Cicero a short way into the Great Hall, where light shone through narrow windows upon a red rug, and upon King John of Saxony, who sat on an elevated throne at the other end. He looked evenly at the knight, and Cicero bowed.

“My king, this is Cicero the Silent. He seeks to do good and serve.”

The King spoke, and his voice was still and short.

“No family name thou giveth, Cicero.”

Cicero was silent.

“Well, we do not need one. Thy arrival is of great fortune for the Saxons. We have a quest for thee, if to do good and serve thou seeketh indeed.” At this he sighed. The knight nodded, and his face betrayed no anticipation.

“Of late a great dragon has come from the Aspenesse.” (For that is what the peoples of the East called that vast, high, dense country between the Saxon hills and the fertile plains east of Tol, which the Tolmen still today call the Cliffs of Fate.) “His true name we ken not, but our people do call him Nog. Already twenty Saxon warriors have met in his jaws a gruesome doom. Renown thou shouldst win indeed if by thy hand this beast is slain. Wouldst thou do this, Silent One?”

Cicero nodded. The King smiled.

“Very well. Search this kingdom, Cicero, and stop not until thou findest it. We would fain send with thee a host of our finest men, but to track such a beast a gentler foot is needed. Take with thee any blade thou desirest from these castle stores.”

He rose to dismiss the knight, but paused.

“We will send thee with a magician of our court,” added the King, “by whom we may tell of any fell tidings that concern thee. Her name is Aquila. She will come to thee in the form of a hawk. Be not alarmed.”

Cicero nodded, though the news was strange to him.

“Farewell. Our steward can help thee if thou needest a sword, or a bed tonight.”

The knight bowed again, and the King left. The steward led him to the armory, talking as they walked.

“Ye are noble indeed if ye would kill this beast, my lord. The common folk do suffer terribly on its account.” He frowned. “There is little barley sown this spring but has been burned and trampled.”

So Cicero set out, taking with him no blade but his own, for in those days Saxon blades were forged stronger and heavier than the blades of his home, and the knight preferred to be quick with his hands.

Another fortnight he wandered that country. Many storms did shake the land, nights filled with the wild terror of the half-forgotten forest, and Cicero was ever watchful. But no dragon did he find, though as he ventured south and east he saw more fields and villages, many scorched, and their people fearful of strangers.

Every few days, the hawk-woman Aquila came to him. Her approach was always sudden, and her message short. The first time she appeared to him it was early morning on the third day of his travels. He was kneeling, packing up his tent.

"Cicero."

At once he lept up and reached for his sword.

"Fear not. I am Aquila, a messenger from the King."

Cicero turned quickly, and behold, there stood in the tall wet grass a hawk, with tattered wings and storm-dark eyes.

“The creature was east of this place but twelve hours ago. Make haste for the river Loyne, for not overlong will he linger.”

He nodded, and the hawk flew away. Cicero saddled his horse and sped east along the old road to the river Loyne. Ere evening he reached it, but the dragon had already left.

So he wandered until the moon was once again almost needle-slim. Aquila appeared to him often, and earnestly he hurried after her reports, urgent tales of a reckless and wanton creature who delighted only in destruction and misery. But though his horse was a great steed of Tol, and tired not easily, yet he was never able to meet the dragon ere it fled. It was a wearisome pursuit back and forth through a land of strange magic and few roads.

Soon, though, the hawk-woman stopped appearing. When five days of her absence had passed, Cicero was troubled, and did resolve to return to the King’s Village. He was encamped on a hill in the east of Saxony, a smoother and less forested country than the rest of the kingdom, and he could see before him many barren fields and fences.

Though the springtime rain had driven Cicero into his tent many other nights, this night the sky was clear, and the beauty of the great multitudes of stars in the open sky did entice Cicero to forsake his shelter, and lay his mat upon the ground. He slept quickly.

He woke up no less quickly, for he was roused by the heavy beating of terrible black wings far above him. With a shout he grabbed his sword and shook his horse, who awoke faster even than the knight, and at once they made chase. Over the great thumping of his steed’s hooves he discerned not the sound of the dragon’s flight, but he marked that evil place in the sky where its wings covered the stars, and followed it. Even so, he lost track of the beast no few times during his pursuit, yet his companion slowed not, for the valiant steed of Tol scented the battle coming anon, and hastened thither, glad for an end to its strange and terrible horse-dreams. Nimbly Cicero sped across ruined half-fenced fields and past abandoned plows.

Eventually the knight came to a small hamlet, where the dragon slowed. It began to fly in a circle. There were a few ragged farmers standing in the street, grouped tightly together, wielding hunting knives and quarterstaffs, and more coming out of houses with torches or shields of wood and leather. They were greatly frightened, but terror overtook them not, and they faced the dragon as it circled them. Cicero stood on the outskirts of the village, which had no fence nor palisade.

At once, one of the villagefolk espied the knight atop his horse.

“Go hence, Angel of Death! Thou hast no companions here!” he cried out, and his voice was tense and shrill.

“Be quiet!” a woman’s voice said. “Who art thou, stranger? Help us!”

Cicero raised his sword, which gleamed orange in the firelight, and turned toward the dragon. The beast’s shadow did seem to pause in flight, and for a moment it hesitated. Then, like a great flood, it came upon them with a fell delight.

Little is known of this battle, by far the greatest and most valorous deed of Cicero the Silent’s early travels. The runaway knight of Tol fought with such bravery and skill as is but rarely seen among mortal men. It is oft said that the fight would have been a longer one but for the courage of the Saxon villagefolk, and though many men and women were injured, few were lost. Valorous especially was the woman called Edith the Brave, who was of great renown in that hamlet, and skillful of speech.

At last, after much battle, the Silent Knight did hew the dragon’s head from its neck with a quick stroke of his blade, and his sword dripping with thick black blood he kneeled for a moment on the ground, and rested. The night was silent, and the stars did seem to rest alongside Cicero the Dragon-Slayer, as the people of the village gathered themselves.

After a short time, a small crowd did approach him. He stood up.

Knight, what do ye call yourself?” spoke Edith. “I should tell my daughters of you ere they are grown.”

Cicero paused for a moment, then took up his sword, which was heavy with blood like black candle-wax. He wrote in the dirt at his feet,

Tell your daughters of the knight called Cicero, but tell them also of the bravery of their mother, who is dearer.

But the Saxon peasantfolk were a people not mindful of the tongues of Tol in which he wrote, and though some of them had seen such script before, yet not one could understand it. As the sun began to rise above the fields, the silent knight bade them a swift and wordless farewell.

Their camp was undisturbed, and man and beast alike slept until midday. When Cicero awoke, he packed hastily, and departed at once for the King’s Village. It was not a short journey, but it was a victorious one, and the hours passed quickly on familiar roads. Three days later, he came again to the great stone tower in the forest.

“Aye, traveller,” The guardsman said to him as he approached. “Hast thou… ah, my lord Cicero! I hope ye will tarry awhile. The young Eliot shall be crowned tomorrow at afternoon. ‘Tis a grand occasion, though a sad one.”

Cicero stared blankly at the guardsman, who looked away uncomfortably.

"My lord."

Cicero was silent. The guardsman continued.

“King John died three nights ago, just ere sunrise.”

The knight went through the gate into the village, and indeed, the narrow windows of the tower were draped with black and red cloth, and the banner was lowered.

Though Cicero was verily praised for his victory, it was a muted welcome, for beloved King John had been by his people, and much renowned for his fairness and even temper. On a bier in the great hall that evening the knights of Saxony did place the body of the old king, with a great red cloak around his neck, and no man that stood there that day dared repeat the terrible rumor they each had heard, that the king in his last hours had despaired of ever ridding his land of the beast that had despoiled it, that in his anguish he had taken up a nimble knife and cut open his neck, and that the young Eliot had found his father thus slain by his own hand – no man dared speak that tale, but each watched the steward strike a match and place it to the body’s beard, and each watched the fire consume it. It was the Saxon custom for kings to burn thus, and not to be buried, as ordinary men were in those days.

And so it came to pass that the young King Eliot took the throne of Saxony, and though he wore the crown even ere his fifteenth birthday, and he was on occasion prone to dark and ominous moods; yet he was a nobler man than his father, and his reign was long and prosperous.

Epilogue.

Cicero sat in his chamber for a time that evening in somber thought, and the events of his days in Saxony did trouble him greatly. Ere long there came a shy knock at his door. The knight’s anxious feet hastened thither.

Standing at Cicero’s door was a young man, with more grace than pride, though when the door was opened he met the great knight’s eyes before he kneeled-–

“My lord Cicero. My name is Aelfhere son of Aeldred. I admire you very much, my lord. I would fain be your squire, and companion, if ye would accept my pledge.”

Cicero stood facing the boy, who after speaking remained on one knee and motionless. He put a hand on Aelfhere’s shoulder, then withdrew into his chamber, leaving the door open. Aelfhere stood up nervously. Cicero returned with a scrap of parchment.

I thank thee, young Aelfhere, and do humbly accept thy pledge.

Then Aelfhere’s heart did tremble, and kneeling once more, he said,

“Then, lord Cicero, I promise to serve you, and in all your good works to be your faithful companion.”

Cicero with trepidation offered the young man his hand, and he kissed it, and rose to leave.

“May I meet you here at dawn, my lord?”

Cicero nodded. Aelfhere bowed once more and left him.

These events were of much private joy to Cicero, and of no little wonder, and he slept for but a few hours that night, marvelling greatly.

Cicero awoke before dawn, and began to pack his belongings, for he meant to saddle his horse and leave Saxony that morning. When the sun had risen he opened his door. Indeed, Aelfhere stood expectant in the hall, carrying a large knapsack, and wearing a sword at his belt. Cicero nodded, and walked out of his chamber, and the door behind him he shut.

Cicero the Silent and Aelfhere son of Aeldred left the King’s Village that morning, with the castle windows draped in black and crimson and the young King Eliot yet uncrowned. As they passed through the silent gates Aelfhere spoke.

“My lord–” he faltered. “Would ye have me take a vow akin to yours?”

Cicero regarded the other young man atop the other horse, and shook his head.

“Very well, my lord,” Aelfhere said. His face showed clearly that regret and relief which are indeed oft akin. So it was that Aelfhere the Brave joined the company of Cicero the Silent, and the two rarely parted from thence onward.
